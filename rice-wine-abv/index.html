Rice Wine ABV

Rice wine never has a starting gravity. Koji or nuruk turns starch into sugar while the yeast is already turning that sugar into alcohol, so there is no single moment when all the sugar is in the tank. Measure the finished wine instead. Pick a method that uses the instruments you own; you will get alcohol and sweetness together.

Hydrometer + refractometer

Alcohol makes a hydrometer read lower but makes a refractometer read higher. Sugar raises both. Two readings of the same wine pin down both unknowns.

How to measure
  1. Degas the sample. Pour it back and forth between two cups, or shake it in a half-full closed bottle and vent, until it stops fizzing. Bubbles cling to the hydrometer and make it float high.
  2. Let the lees settle, or strain through a coffee filter, and use the clear liquid.
  3. Float the hydrometer, spin it to knock off bubbles, and read where the flat liquid surface crosses the scale. Note the sample's temperature.
  4. Check the refractometer reads 0.0 with plain water, then put 2–3 drops of the same sample on the prism and read the Brix.

Boil-off test

Read the wine, boil the alcohol away, add water back to the original amount and read again. The second sample has the same sugar but no alcohol, so the difference between the two readings is the alcohol. Wine labs call this Tabarié's rule.

How to measure
  1. Degas and clear the sample (stir out the fizz, let the lees settle or filter). Read the wine with your hydrometer, refractometer, or both.
  2. Put a small pot on a kitchen scale, tare it, and pour in about 250 g of the wine. Write down the weight. Without a scale, measure exactly 250 mL instead.
  3. Simmer gently, uncovered, until about half the liquid has evaporated. That drives off more than 99 % of the alcohol. Keep the heat low so nothing scorches.
  4. Cool it to room temperature. Add clean water (distilled, or boiled and cooled) until it is back to the starting weight or volume, and stir well.
  5. Read the topped-up sample with the same instrument(s).

Boiling point

Alcohol lowers the boiling point: a 12 % wine boils about 8.6 °C below water. Boiling plain water first calibrates your thermometer and today's air pressure, so altitude and weather cancel out. This is how an ebulliometer works.

How to measure
  1. Use the same thermometer, pot and heat setting for both tests. A narrow pot or jar works best. A lid with a hole for the probe helps, and a 50 cm tube through the lid acts as a condenser that keeps the alcohol in.
  2. Boil plain water and write down the steady boiling temperature.
  3. Pour out the water and boil at least 200 mL of degassed wine. Read the lowest steady temperature in the first minute of boiling. It slowly creeps up as the alcohol escapes.
  4. Keep the probe tip in the liquid and off the bottom of the pot.
  5. If thick or very sweet wine foams or scorches, mix it 1:1 with water and set the dilution above.

Distill + alcoholmeter

An alcoholmeter only works in plain alcohol and water. Floated in wine, the sugar makes it read far too low. Distilling a measured sample moves all the alcohol into clean water, where the alcoholmeter reads it correctly. This is the official lab reference method.

How to measure
  1. Measure 100 mL of degassed wine at room temperature in a measuring cylinder. Pour it into the still and rinse the cylinder into it with a splash of water.
  2. Distill slowly back into the same cylinder until you have collected about three-quarters of the starting volume. All the alcohol comes over in that part.
  3. Top the distillate up with water to the starting volume and mix. Collecting into a smaller final volume, say 50 mL, doubles the reading and makes a coarse alcoholmeter more precise; enter both volumes.
  4. Float the alcoholmeter, read it at the flat liquid surface, and note the temperature.

    Why the hydrometer's alcohol scale can't help

    The "potential alcohol" scale on a triple-scale hydrometer assumes you read it before fermentation started. With rice wine, sugar is made and eaten at the same time, so that reading never exists.

    Cloudy wine

    Makgeolli, tapai, nigori and other unfiltered styles carry rice solids. They don't change the alcohol, but they make hydrometers float high and blur refractometer lines. Let the jar settle in the fridge, or filter a sample, then bring it back to room temperature.

    Temperature

    Read hydrometers and alcoholmeters as close to their calibration temperature as you can. The app corrects the difference with the same formula used for the international alcohol tables, but readings between 15 and 25 °C are the most reliable.

    Vinometers

    Capillary vinometers only work in dry, clear wine. Sugar changes the surface tension they rely on, so they read badly wrong in most rice wines.
